What just happened

    For the curious

    The whole method is open source, so anyone can read exactly what happened to your link. Released models are frozen, so a furl made today still unfurls in twenty years.

    What this is

    A link shortener that forgot to build the database. Instead of saving your URL somewhere and handing you a ticket number, enfurl compresses the URL and hands you the result. It all happens on your device.

    So: no sign-up, no dashboard, no expiry, and no way for anyone (us included) to look up who clicked what. A furl also outlives this site, because the open source tools unfurl it offline.

    Why it comes out short

    Real links are predictable. A model trained on a million shared links already expects https://www., knows the common sites, and has seen /watch?v= a hundred thousand times. Those parts cost almost nothing to write down.

    What no tool can help with is randomness. A video id or a token is random on purpose, so it costs its full size everywhere. The breakdown above shows which of your characters were free and which were not.

    The words

    furl verb, 16th century
    What sailors do to a sail: roll it up tight against the spar. Nothing is cut away, it is only made small, and any sailor can shake it out again. The other reading of f + url came free with the name, and we are keeping it.
    enfurl /ɪnˈfɜːrl/
    To furl in; to wrap. Tolkien used it for mountains: "in silence folded, mist-enfurled". We use it for links, and the word happens to carry url in the middle of it.
    a furl noun, ours
    A URL rolled up. The thing you copy and send.
    unfurl
    To open a furl back into the link it was made from. Also what chat apps do when they expand a link into a preview. Everyone may unfurl; nobody needs our permission.
    furl.li
    Where furls live. Read it as furly.

    Honest limits

    • A furl cannot be edited, expired, or revoked. Nothing is stored, so there is nothing to change.
    • Very random links barely shrink. No tool can beat that; we just show you where it happens.
    • A furl is rolled, not locked: whoever holds it holds the link. Secrets need a different tool.
    • A link that is mostly a signed token (a magic link, an SSO link, a JWT) is mostly random by design. We open the readable parts and compress those, so expect around a third off, not nine tenths. The fix for those is a smaller token, not a cleverer compressor.
    • With furl.li/… our redirect receives the furl. It logs nothing, and you can check, because it is the same open code running here. With furl.li/#… it never receives it at all.
    The maths, briefly

    Compression has a hard floor, and it is not an engineering limit, it is arithmetic. If a model thinks a piece of text has probability p, writing that piece down costs log2(1/p) bits. Nothing can do better; that quantity is called the information content, and it is why the numbers above are in bits rather than characters.

    Two consequences run this whole tool. First, expected things are almost free: if the model gives /watch?v= a probability of 0.93 after seeing youtube.com, writing it costs 0.10 bits, not 72. Second, surprises cost full price: an eleven-character YouTube id has 64 equally likely options per character, so it costs 11 × log2(64) = 66 bits, and no amount of cleverness reduces that. A furl is written in the 64 characters A-Z a-z 0-9 - _, so each character carries 6 bits, and the length you see is simply the total divided by six.

    The model is a static order-2 context model: for every pair of characters it has seen, it keeps the odds of what comes next, learned once from a million real links and then frozen. The encoder picks the cheapest way to describe your link (character by character, or by a phrase it recognises, or as a run of random-looking data) using a shortest-path search, and an arithmetic coder writes the result at fractional bits per symbol, which is what lets a near-certain piece cost a tenth of a bit. The whole method is in the source, comments and all.
